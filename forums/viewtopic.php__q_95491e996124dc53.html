Jerky movement in mulitplayer: Lag, Ping rates & You.

by stimarco on Sun Sep 02, 2007 6:48 am

This FAQ answers the following questions:

1. Why are my opponents' planes jerking or moving weirdly?
2. What is "Lag"?
3. What purpose does the "Ping" rate serve?

Jerkiness is caused by "network lag". (Often referred to, erroneously, as "Ping" rate.)

This is basically the time it takes for a message to get from the source to its destination over a network. The internet is an SUV rather than a sports car: it was designed to be robust, first and foremost. Speed was a secondary factor.

When a message is sent over the internet, it may be sent via a direct route -- maybe a couple of hops -- or it may be sent on a more circuitous route, say via a satellite uplink. Each hop adds a few milliseconds to the journey time.

An example: I'm based int he UK. If I were to access a server in, say, China, my request might be routed via points in Europe and Russia, but there are a lot of countries in Europe, so that's a fair few hops in its own right. Alternatively, my request might go via Canada -- perhaps just two hops -- and reach China far more quickly. The important point is that *I* don't get to choose which route the data takes: the internet does.

It is this journey time which causes problems with network games played over the internet. This journey time is called Lag.

Your computer might send a message -- "Player pressed fire button. He's at _these_ coordinates and is facing _that_ direction moving at _this_ velocity." This message must first get to the server which is where the game rules are processed. The server receives this message a dozen milliseconds later, processes it and informs all the connected computers what the result was. E.g. "Player 1 shot Player 4. Player 4 was killed."

Unfortunately, a few dozen milliseconds' lag is fast for the internet. For some players, it may take a couple of hundred milliseconds for their messages to make the trip. That's a long time -- if every player was forced to wait for all these messages to arrive and be sent back, you'd be lucky to see maybe 5-6 display updates per second.

The solution to this is interpolation. The server interpolates moves -- basically forecasting each player's likely movements over time -- and sends these guesses out to each computer. The messages that come in to the server are then used to synchronise and refine its forecasts. The shorter the lag, the less likely the server is to make wildly inaccurate guesses as a lot can happen in even a quarter of a second.

Think of this as the difference between forecasting the weather accurately over five days, versus looking out the window and deciding whether you should take an umbrella with you.

The upshot is that you will see increasingly inaccurate guesses if there's a player with a high-lag connection involved in your gaming session. That jerkiness, the odd spinning planes and the occasional "Hey! He wasn't anywhere near me when he fired! How come he killed me?" reaction is entirely due to inaccurate forecasting on the part of the server.

So where does "Ping" come into this?

"Ping" is a venerable network diagnostic program designed to test an internet (or other network) connection. Each message sent over a network is automatically time-stamped and Ping takes advantage of this to work out the time taken for the test message to arrive at its destination. (Almost all modern computers have this program installed.)

Some network servers use "ping rates" as a measure of lag. This measurement is usually a pretty good indicator of lag time between your computer and the host computer. Other players will see different ping rates, depending on their physical location relative to the server.

The longer your lag, the higher your ping rate, the worse your gaming experience will be. If you're only seeing a high ping rate for specific game servers, try looking for servers closer to you, or connected to the same ISP. If you are seeing high ping rates and long lag times generally, you should talk to your ISP provider.
