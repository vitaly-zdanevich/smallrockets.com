Hi CdnGhost.

I think there's some confusion here. The speed of your cable modem only tells you how fast it can send and receive packets of data. Unfortunately, the Internet does not provide simple point-to-point connections: it's a "network of networks". When you send a packet of data over the internet, it has to travel across any number of networks to get to its destination. If there's a lot of congestion along the most direct route, each packet may end up diverted over a much longer distance.

(Contrary to popular belief, the internet was designed for robustness, not raw speed. It was considered more important that data gets from A to B. Not that it does so <i>quickly</i>.)

At each stop along the way, the packet effectively asks each stop "Where should I go next?" If there's a direct route, there shouldn't be too many such stops, but if the direct route is busy, the data may have to take a long diversion.

It's this traveling process which causes "lag". The term refers to the time it takes for a data packet to be received. The lower the lag -- usually measured as the "ping rate" -- the quicker your data is reaching the other end. (And vice-versa.)

The rated speed of your connection merely dictates how quickly you can send and receive the data in those packets. Having a fast car doesn't help you any if you're stuck in traffic.
