Anonymous wrote:
hey guys whats up with MAD 2?
i would like to buy MAD 1 but 15 bucks?... well come on, make it free ware !


Remember: we aren't paying a middleman or publisher, or cluttering up a warehouse or shelf-space with copies of the game. The running costs of our website and other overheads aren't going down. Internet bandwidth costs serious cash.

Making a game free takes a fair bit of effort at the moment. We need to change the installer and the game's help files, update the website manually -- it's all done manually, there's no content management system in place here -- and fiddle with the license code generation back-end server to make it produce freebie keys. It also means tweaking the database as well.

In other words: it ain't as easy as it seems. The recent 'freebification' of the old "D-Inter" titles we used to sell for money took nearly two weeks out of RAS 2 development. (And those games are only free because we literally can't make any changes to 'em: we don't have the source code.)

We can't really justify another delay to that at this point, but we will be re-evaluating our entire back-catalogue once RAS 2 is finished. It's more likely that we'd lower the prices on our old games; as they generally work fine, there's no pressing reason to make them free.

That said, a sequel to MAD makes sense to me. I even think it'd do well in the Mac OS X market too, although when such a sequel can be squeezed into Jon's schedule is another matter.

rember911 wrote:
I highly agree that mad shuold be made freeware. i mean, first of all, how many people actually will still be buying that game?


Quite a few, actually. Hell, I'm still amazed at how often I get support requests for Art Is Dead -- a game designed for _light guns!_

In fact, all our games have sold steadily throughout their lives. Not everybody owns a top-of-the-line Windows XP PC with seriously powerful graphics cards, so why should we cater only to people who have the latest and greatest kit?

A PC that was current when MAD was released will still run MAD perfectly happily today.

The only reason games have had a limited shelf-life traditionally is because typical retail stores only have a limited amount of shelf _space_ to use for selling them. Traditional publishers rely on these stores to sell their wares, but this has resulted in an insane situation today whereby a game that may have taken 30 people three _years_ to make, costing millions of dollars, gets -- wait for it! -- a mere _two weeks_ in a retail store to see if it'll sell. If it doesn't sell above a certain number of units in that time, the remaining stock is sent back to the publisher for disposal, or the games are tossed into the "Buy 1, Get One Free!" bargain bins.

That's why we sell our games online and nowhere else. We don't need to worry about shelf space and shelf-lives. If nobody wants to play one of our games, they won't download it. If they're not downloading it, they're not using up any of our bandwidth. We're not losing any money by keeping it available. We lose money only if people frequently download a game, but don't buy it -- usually because the game doesn't run on their system. We had this problem with the "D-Inter"-developed games, which were impossible to patch.

Our other games are patchable. The only problem is that we need to devote time and resources to examining each title to see if it's (a) worth patching, or (b) worth making available for free. (b) isn't an option if it means we'll have all our bandwidth wiped out by people downloading the freebies.
